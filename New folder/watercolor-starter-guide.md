## Section 1 — Understanding Watercolor

Watercolor is unique among painting mediums because **water is your primary tool**, not the paint. Every mistake beginners make traces back to not understanding this single fact.

### How Watercolor Actually Works

Watercolor paint is pigment suspended in a water-soluble binder called gum arabic. When you add water, the pigment becomes mobile. When the water evaporates, the pigment is locked into the paper's surface permanently.

This means:
- **More water = lighter, more transparent color**
- **Less water = darker, more saturated color**
- **Once dry, watercolor cannot be fully removed**

### The Three States of Watercolor Paper

Learning to read the wetness of your paper is the most important skill in watercolor.

| State | Appearance | What Happens When You Add Paint |
|-------|-----------|--------------------------------|
| Wet | Shiny surface, visible water | Paint spreads and blooms freely |
| Damp | Slightly shiny, matte patches | Paint spreads softly, edges blur |
| Dry | Completely matte | Paint stays exactly where you put it |

> **Pro tip:** Tilt your paper toward a light source to check wetness. If it shines, it is still wet. If it is completely matte, it is dry. The damp stage is the trickiest to judge and the most useful to master.

## Section 2 — Your Materials

### Paper

Paper is the most important material in watercolor. Do not skip on paper quality.

**Recommended for beginners:**
- Arches 140lb Cold Press — the industry standard
- Fabriano Artistico 140lb Cold Press — excellent and slightly cheaper
- Strathmore 400 Series Watercolor — good student grade option

**What to avoid:**
- Copy paper or printer paper — paint will buckle immediately
- Spiral bound student sketchbooks — paper is too thin
- Hot press paper — too slippery for beginners

**Paper weight explained:**
- 90lb — too thin, will buckle badly
- 140lb — the standard, stretches a little but workable
- 300lb — professional grade, no stretching needed

### Paints

You do not need many colors to start. The following twelve colors will mix nearly any color you need:

**Warm colors:**
1. Cadmium Yellow (or Hansa Yellow as a non-toxic alternative)
2. Yellow Ochre
3. Cadmium Orange
4. Cadmium Red (or Pyrrol Red)
5. Burnt Sienna
6. Burnt Umber

**Cool colors:**
7. Lemon Yellow
8. Quinacridone Magenta
9. Ultramarine Blue
10. Cerulean Blue
11. Viridian Green
12. Payne's Gray

**Recommended brands by budget:**

| Budget | Brand | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| Under $30 | Cotman by Winsor & Newton | Reliable student grade |
| $30–$60 | Van Gogh by Royal Talens | Strong student/artist hybrid |
| $60+ | Winsor & Newton Artists | Professional grade, worth it |

### Brushes

You need far fewer brushes than you think.

**The essential three:**
- **Large round (size 12–14):** For washes and large areas
- **Medium round (size 8):** Your workhorse brush for most painting
- **Small round (size 2–4):** For details and fine lines

**What makes a good watercolor brush:**
- Holds a good amount of water (belly capacity)
- Snaps back to a fine point when wet
- Releases paint smoothly

**Recommended brands:**
- Princeton Neptune Series (synthetic, great value)
- Winsor & Newton Cotman (synthetic, affordable)
- Da Vinci Casalon (synthetic, excellent)

### Other Materials

- **Two water containers** — one for clean water, one for rinsing
- **Palette** — ceramic or plastic with wells, at least 12 mixing spaces
- **Masking tape or gummed tape** — to tape paper to a board
- **Wooden board or thick foam board** — to mount your paper
- **Paper towels or a clean rag** — for lifting paint and correcting

## Section 3 — Core Techniques

### Technique 1 — The Flat Wash

A flat wash is an even, consistent area of color with no variation.

**How to do it:**
1. Mix a generous amount of paint — more than you think you need
2. Tilt your board to about 15 degrees
3. Load your largest brush fully with paint
4. Make a horizontal stroke across the top of your area
5. Pick up the bead of paint that collects at the bottom of your stroke
6. Make another horizontal stroke immediately below, slightly overlapping
7. Repeat until the area is covered
8. At the bottom, absorb the final bead with a barely damp brush

**Common mistakes:**
- Running out of paint mid-wash — always mix more than you need
- Going back into a drying wash — causes blooms and hard edges
- Uneven pressure — keep your stroke consistent

---

### Technique 2 — The Graded Wash

A graded wash transitions smoothly from dark to light or from one color to another.

**Dark to light graded wash:**
1. Start with a fully loaded brush of your darkest value
2. Paint the first stroke
3. Dip your brush in clean water and add a small amount to your palette mix
4. Paint the next stroke
5. Repeat, adding more water each time
6. The color becomes progressively lighter and more transparent

**Color to color graded wash:**
1. Start with Color A fully loaded
2. Gradually introduce Color B into your palette mix
3. By the halfway point your mix should be mostly Color B
4. End with pure Color B

---

### Technique 3 — Wet on Wet

Wet on wet means applying wet paint to a wet surface. This creates soft, diffused edges and unpredictable blooms.

**How to do it:**
1. Wet your paper with clean water using a large brush
2. Wait until the shine begins to dull slightly (damp stage)
3. Load your brush with paint and touch it to the damp surface
4. Watch the paint spread on its own
5. You can guide it gently but resist overworking

**What wet on wet is good for:**
- Soft atmospheric skies
- Misty backgrounds
- Soft shadow areas
- Flowers and organic forms

---

### Technique 4 — Wet on Dry

Wet on dry means applying wet paint to completely dry paper. This creates hard, crisp edges.

**What wet on dry is good for:**
- Architectural details
- Sharp horizon lines
- Precise shapes
- Layering and glazing

---

### Technique 5 — Glazing

Glazing is applying a transparent layer of paint over a completely dry layer to modify its color or value.

**Rules of glazing:**
- The underlayer must be completely dry — wait at least 20 minutes
- Use a light touch — scrubbing will lift the underlayer
- Transparent pigments glaze best (Quinacridone, Phthalo, Ultramarine)
- Opaque pigments (Cadmium, Cerulean) are harder to glaze with

**What glazing is good for:**
- Deepening shadows without muddying
- Shifting the temperature of a passage
- Creating luminous color through layers

---

### Technique 6 — Lifting

Lifting means removing wet or damp paint from the paper to create lighter areas or correct mistakes.

**Methods:**
- **Dry brush lifting:** Touch a dry brush to wet paint to absorb it
- **Tissue lifting:** Press a crumpled tissue to wet paint for texture
- **Kneaded eraser lifting:** On dry paint, a kneaded eraser can lift some pigment
- **Wet lifting:** Wet a dry passage and scrub gently with a stiff brush

> **Important:** How much paint lifts depends on the pigment. Staining pigments (Phthalo Blue, Quinacridone) barely lift. Non-staining pigments (Ultramarine, Burnt Sienna) lift much more easily. Know your pigments.

## Section 4 — Color Mixing

### The Color Mixing Chart

Before you paint anything else, make a color mixing chart. Take every color on your palette and mix it with every other color in small squares on a piece of watercolor paper. Label each square. This chart becomes your reference for the rest of your painting life.

### Why Colors Go Muddy

Colors turn muddy when you mix too many pigments together. Three or more pigments in a single mix almost always produce a gray or brown result.

**The rule:** Mix a maximum of two colors together. If you need a third, make sure one of the original two is transparent.

### Essential Color Mixing Recipes

| Color I Want | Mix These |
|---|---|
| Bright orange | Cadmium Yellow + Cadmium Red |
| Warm green | Lemon Yellow + Ultramarine Blue |
| Cool green | Lemon Yellow + Cerulean Blue |
| Purple | Quinacridone Magenta + Ultramarine Blue |
| Warm gray | Burnt Umber + Ultramarine Blue |
| Dark neutral | Burnt Sienna + Ultramarine Blue (vary ratio for warm or cool) |
| Skin tone base | Yellow Ochre + Cadmium Red (tiny amount) |

### Mixing Blacks and Darks

Never use tube black straight from the tube. It deadens everything around it.

**Better darks:**
- Ultramarine Blue + Burnt Sienna (warm dark)
- Ultramarine Blue + Burnt Umber (cooler dark)
- Phthalo Blue + Quinacridone Magenta + Yellow (chromatic black)

## Section 5 — Your First Three Projects

### Project 1 — Simple Sky and Horizon (30 minutes)

**What you will practice:** Flat wash, graded wash, wet on wet

1. Tape a half sheet of watercolor paper to your board
2. Wet the entire paper with clean water
3. While still wet, paint a graded wash of Cerulean Blue from the top, fading out by the middle of the paper
4. Let dry completely
5. Mix a warm green from Lemon Yellow and Ultramarine Blue
6. Paint a flat wash across the bottom half of the paper for land
7. Add a darker mix of trees along the horizon line while the land wash is still damp

---

### Project 2 — Single Flower (45 minutes)

**What you will practice:** Wet on wet for petals, wet on dry for details

1. Lightly sketch a simple five-petal flower in pencil
2. Wet one petal at a time and drop in color while wet
3. Let each petal dry before painting adjacent petals
4. Once all petals are dry, add the center with a darker mix
5. Add stem and leaves with wet on dry strokes
6. Add final dark details with your small round brush

---

### Project 3 — Simple Still Life (1 hour)

**What you will practice:** All techniques combined

Set up a simple arrangement: one piece of fruit, a small cup or jar, and a draped cloth.

1. Sketch lightly in pencil
2. Identify your light source and mark the shadow areas
3. Paint the lightest areas first with thin transparent washes
4. Build up darker values with glazing
5. Paint the cast shadows last with a wet on dry technique
6. Add final details with your small brush

## Section 6 — Common Problems and Solutions

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Paint blooms unexpectedly | Added paint to paper that was wetter than you thought | Wait for paper to reach the damp stage, not wet |
| Hard edges where you wanted soft | Paper was too dry when you added paint | Re-wet the area and work faster |
| Colors look muddy | Too many pigments mixed together | Limit to two pigments per mix |
| Paint lifts when glazing | Underlayer was not fully dry | Wait longer, at least 30 minutes |
| Paper buckles badly | Paper too thin or not taped properly | Use 140lb paper and tape all four edges |
| Colors look dull when dry | Watercolor dries lighter than it looks wet | Mix slightly darker than you want |
| Brush strokes visible in wash | Went back into drying paint | Work faster or let dry and glaze |

## Final Notes

Watercolor rewards patience above all else. The artists who struggle most are the ones who rush. They go back into wet paint, they overwork passages, they do not let layers dry.

The artists who improve fastest are the ones who slow down, observe their paper carefully, and make deliberate decisions.

You have everything you need to start. Set up your materials, tape down a piece of paper, and make your first wash today.

The only way to get better is to paint.
